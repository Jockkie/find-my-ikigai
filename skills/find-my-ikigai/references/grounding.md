# Grounding: what Kamiya found, and the short lines to use

Use these lines after the user answers, at most two sentences, always attributed. Use each line at most once per session, and in Part 4 at most one grounding for the whole part (plus the two need-specific lines). Skip grounding when it would land badly: after a flat or painful answer ("nothing", "I don't know"), acknowledge instead; after "nothing to look forward to", use the small-goals line rather than the future-orientation line. They explain the topic or why a question is asked; they never interpret the user's answer. Translate them into the user's language.

## Sources

Kamiya's *Ikigai ni tsuite* (1966) has not been translated into English. The points below come from secondary sources that quote and summarise it:
- Ikigai Tribe (Nicholas Kemp): "Ikigai Sources & Ikigai-Kan", "The Seven Ikigai Needs", "Ikigai ni Tsuite", podcast interviews with Kei Tsuda and Yoko Inoue (ikigaitribe.com)
- The Meaning Movement, "Ikigai Meaning: What It Actually Is (And Isn't)" (themeaningmovement.com)
- Wikipedia, "Mieko Kamiya" and "Ikigai"

If a user asks for more than these sources support, say that you do not know and that the book is only available in Japanese.

## Who Kamiya was
- Japanese psychiatrist (1914 to 1979) who worked with patients at the Nagashima Aiseien leprosarium from 1957.
- Her central question: what makes a person feel that life is worth living?
- She noticed that some patients with mild physical symptoms suffered deeply from a sense of meaninglessness, while others found meaning in very hard circumstances.

**Line:** "Kamiya studied people who had lost almost everything, and asked what still made their life feel worth living."

## Source and feeling
- Two uses of the word: the source or object ("this child is my ikigai") and ikigai-kan, the state of mind of feeling it. She linked ikigai-kan to Frankl's "sense of meaning".
- Many people hunt for the right source (a calling, a career) and barely notice the feeling when it is there.

**Lines:**
- Before Part 2: "Kamiya made a distinction most ikigai books skip: the source you live for, and the feeling that life is worth living. We start with the feeling."
- Before Part 3: "Now the sources: for Kamiya, a source can be a person, an activity, a ritual or a hope, as long as it brings that feeling."

## Ikigai-kan versus happiness
- Compared to happiness, ikigai-kan has a clearer attitude towards the future, is closer to one's sense of self, and is strongly linked to personal values.
- It can be felt in hardship, through effort and devotion, not only in comfort.
- It is more about what you feel than about what you achieve; some summaries quote her as describing it as joy that comes from deep within, "from the belly".

**Lines:**
- On a moment of effort or difficulty: "Kamiya noticed that this feeling can be strongest in effort or hardship, not only in comfort."
- On values: "For Kamiya the feeling is closely tied to personal values, which is why we looked at yours first."
- On looking forward: "Kamiya saw future orientation as what sets this feeling apart from happiness: having something to move towards."

## The seven needs
- Seven conditions or needs; different people need different combinations; the most consistent ones among the patients she studied: life satisfaction, change and growth, a bright future, resonance, freedom, self-actualisation, meaning and value.
- Life satisfaction she saw as the most fundamental: the sense that life is moving in a better direction. Fear, anxiety and resentment can undermine it.
- A bright future: the expectation that life will unfold in a new direction; she stressed both short-term achievable goals and long-term ambitions.
- She also wrote about a sense of purpose or mission (shimei-kan), positively linked to ikigai-kan, as something beyond a need.

**Lines:**
- Before Part 4: "Kamiya found seven needs behind the feeling; not everyone needs all seven, and each person has their own mix."
- On life satisfaction: "Kamiya saw this as the most basic need: the sense that your life is moving in a better direction."
- On a bright future: "Kamiya stressed small goals you can reach soon as well as long-term ones; both feed this need."
- On resonance, freedom, self-actualisation, meaning: "This is one of the seven needs Kamiya found; we look at it the same way, with a score and a moment."

## Small and plural
- Ikigai can be taking joy in small pleasures as well as pursuing a life-defining goal.
- A spread of several sources, many of them not professional, is more resilient than one.

**Lines:**
- On a small source: "For Kamiya a small daily thing counts just as much as a big mission."
- On the fragility check: "Several sources make the feeling sturdier; if one carries everything, losing it hurts more."

## What is not Kamiya
- The four-circle diagram (love, good at, world needs, paid for) is a later Western adaptation focused on work.
- The owned-or-borrowed exercise with submodalities comes from NLP.

**Lines:**
- Offering Part 5: "This next exercise is not from Kamiya but from NLP; it helps to feel which wishes are yours and which you carry for someone else."
- Offering Part 7: "The four-circle diagram is a Western addition about work; we can use it as a practical check, not as the definition of your ikigai."
